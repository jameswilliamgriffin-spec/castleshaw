You are an Awwwards / FWA jury member and a senior interaction designer. Give a candid, critical review of a homepage design concept, with concrete, prioritised improvements. British English. Be specific; skip generic advice ("add micro-interactions", "improve performance") unless you say exactly what and where.

Client: Castleshaw Centre, Oldham council's outdoor and environmental education centre in Delph, at the edge of the Peak District. Audience: teachers booking school trips, youth leaders, families. The goal is booking enquiries (phone 0161 770 8595).

Brand: the logo is a stack of landscape bands (pale sky #bfe4f7, sage far hill #c1d284, moss near hill #8dba08, a white path line, reservoir blue #0480bc, and a wordmark in #397ca7). Text colour is #0a2a3c (a deep version of the water blue). Fonts: Bricolage Grotesque (display, narrowed with its width axis) and Literata (serif body). Photography is the centre's own, but small (600 to 1,000 px).

Page structure (version 2), top to bottom. The attached screenshots show it at desktop and mobile widths:
1. Hero on a pale-sky band. Headline "Out here, the Pennines are the classroom." rises in word by word. A sun disc follows the pointer across a WebGL sky with soft drifting clouds. Three flat illustrated ridges lean away from the sun at different depths, and three arch-topped photos sit tucked between them. Primary button "Plan your visit", plus the phone number.
2. "Six ways to spend a day": a horizontal card track driven by vertical scroll (pinned). Each card has an arch-masked photo with a WebGL ripple on hover, a title, two lines of copy and an "Add to your plan" toggle that feeds the planner. The band's background colour changes per activity, and a dot index shows progress.
3. The transition: a full-screen pinned illustration of the valley (flat sky, hills, reservoir and sun) with the headline "Same hills. / A little less flat." Underneath sits a canvas whose first frame is the same drawing, generated by AI video. On scroll, the flat layers fade front to back and leave their ridge outlines as chalk-white tracing lines, while the film beneath morphs the drawing into photoreal Pennine footage. Scrolling back up rewinds.
4. A six-chapter scroll-scrubbed film (WebP frames on canvas): Hills, Woods (descending into a birch wood), Mud (low tracking shot of wellies splashing a puddle), Water (into the puddle's reflection and out onto the reservoir from a red kayak), Fire (overhead marshmallows over a fire stove; this lands on the centre's own real photo), Dusk (rising over the valley to lit cottage windows and stars). Content sits over it with side scrims: chapter text; the "Plan your visit" four-step planner card; the real quote "One of the highlights was jumping in puddles!"; a kayaking note; a pile of real children's thank-you drawings you can swipe through; and a "Stay the night" enquiry panel with the big phone number and a form.
5. Persistent extras: a small trail elevation-line chapter marker (top right) with a dot that walks along it, a sticky "Call 0161 770 8595" pill, an ambient-sound toggle (off by default), an "All versions" link, and a header "Enquire" button that counts picked activities.

This has a distinctive visual language and a strong sense of place. The landscape bands, reservoir blues and arches feel connected to Castleshaw. But the experience currently asks visitors to admire and navigate quite a lot before they can confidently arrange a trip. Its strongest material is the centre’s own photography and children’s responses; those deserve more prominence than the cinematic effects.

| Usability | **5.5/10** | Two pinned sequences, hidden activity choices and a planner embedded in moving scenery create friction. |

### B. Eight improvements, ranked

**1. Give teachers a direct enquiry route in the hero.**  
Make “Enquire about a visit” the primary button and “Build a visit plan” secondary. Open a short form with group type, approximate numbers, preferred dates and contact details. Keep the phone alongside. A teacher who already knows what they need should reach an enquiry without completing the experience.

**2. Bring booking confidence above the film.**  
After the hero, add a compact practical strip: age groups served, day/residential options, what staff provide, and a link to access and visit information—all verified. The current introduction describes activities but leaves organisers’ immediate questions unanswered.

**3. Shorten the illustration-to-film transition and remove its headline.**  
“Same hills. A little less flat.” reads as commentary on the design technique. Let the valley transform within roughly one viewport of scrolling, then release into content. Preserve the reservoir and ridge alignment throughout: the recognisable geography should carry the reveal.

**4. Make the transition resolve cleanly into authentic footage.**  
The middle screenshot’s pale wash and white outlines obscure both drawing and landscape. Trace just one ridge briefly, then clear it as the real view arrives. End on a matched centre photograph or genuine footage; avoid lingering through an AI morph where terrain appears to liquefy.

**5. Replace the pinned activity conveyor with immediately visible choices.**  
Show six compact activity cards in a desktop grid and a normal vertical mobile list. Expand a selected card for detail and “Add to plan”. In screenshot 2, most of the screen exposes only one activity and a faded fragment of another; comparing a day’s options becomes unnecessarily slow.

**6. Give the planner a stable surface and a useful finish.**  
Open it in a drawer from the hero and activity cards, rather than making Woods its required destination. Finish with an editable summary and “Enquire about this plan”, carrying selections into the form. Replace the header’s unexplained count with “Your plan · 3 activities”.

**7. Untangle the persistent controls.**  
Remove “All versions” from the public page. Make the trail marker clickable with readable chapter names, or remove it. On mobile, place the call action in a slim bottom bar with safe spacing; the three floating pills currently dominate the lower edge.

**8. Give the children’s work room to be read.**  
Reduce “They sent us these” and show one complete drawing or letter with its attribution, plus explicit previous/next controls. Screenshot 8 crops the evidence beneath an enormous heading. These artefacts provide more persuasive proof than another cinematic chapter.

### C. What should go

The AI valley journey risks implying documentary authenticity, particularly the invented cottage lights and stars. Remove visibly synthetic shots. Drop the hover ripple: distorting small photographs weakens their credibility. The repeated arches work initially but become a formula. Keep them selectively. Replace “They sent us these” with the more concrete “Back at school, they wrote to us”.

### D. A signature idea, achievable in a day

**Turn one real pupil drawing into the landscape transition.** Match its hill outline to a centre photograph, then dissolve between them on a short scroll. Credit the pupil and school, with permission. It would make the visual transformation about what children notice and remember—and give a juror a specific Castleshaw moment to recall.
